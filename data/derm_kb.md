# Dermatology Quick Reference – Common Skin Conditions

## Acne vulgaris
Clinical features: Open and closed comedones, inflammatory papules, pustules, nodules, and possible scarring. Most common on face, chest, and back. Affects adolescents and young adults.
Diagnostic clues: Comedones are pathognomonic. No itching unless irritated. Differentiate from rosacea (absence of comedones, central facial erythema, telangiectasias).
Standard management: First line: topical retinoid + benzoyl peroxide. Moderate: add topical antibiotic (clindamycin). Severe or scarring: oral isotretinoin. Refer if refractory after 3 months.

## Actinic keratosis
Clinical features: Rough, scaly macule or papule on sun‑exposed skin (face, scalp, hands). Typically 2–6 mm, skin‑colored to reddish‑brown, often felt before seen. More common in fair‑skinned elderly.
Diagnostic clues: Gritty texture on palpation. Sun‑damaged surrounding skin. May be tender. Biopsy if thick, ulcerated, or rapidly growing to rule out squamous cell carcinoma.
Standard management: Cryotherapy for solitary lesions. Field therapy (5‑FU, imiquimod, photodynamic therapy) for multiple lesions. Sun protection mandatory.

## Atopic dermatitis (eczema)
Clinical features: Intensely pruritic, ill‑defined erythematous patches and plaques, often with lichenification from chronic rubbing. In infants: face and extensor surfaces. In adults: flexural areas (antecubital, popliteal).
Diagnostic clues: Pruritus is essential for diagnosis. Personal or family history of atopy. Dry skin (xerosis). Distinguish from psoriasis (well‑demarcated, silvery scale, less itch).
Standard management: Emollients, topical corticosteroids (potency based on severity), calcineurin inhibitors for sensitive areas. Dupilumab for moderate‑to‑severe refractory disease.

## Basal cell carcinoma
Clinical features: Pearly, translucent papule with telangiectasias, rolled border, and central ulceration (rodent ulcer). Most common on head and neck. Slow growing, rarely metastasizes.
Diagnostic clues: History of intermittent bleeding. May appear as flat, scar‑like plaque (morpheaform subtype). Nodular BCC is classic. Biopsy confirms.
Standard management: Surgical excision with 4 mm margins. Mohs micrographic surgery for high‑risk sites or large lesions. Topical imiquimod for superficial BCC in low‑risk areas.

## Melanoma
Clinical features: Asymmetrical, irregularly bordered, multicolored macule or nodule, diameter >6 mm (ABCDE rule). May arise de novo or from existing nevus. Can occur anywhere, including palms, soles, and nails.
Diagnostic clues: Evolution (change in size, shape, color) is most sensitive sign. Dermoscopy shows atypical pigment network, irregular streaks, blue‑white veil.
Standard management: Wide local excision with margins per Breslow depth. Sentinel lymph node biopsy for tumors >1 mm depth. Systemic immunotherapy/targeted therapy for metastatic disease.

## Psoriasis
Clinical features: Well‑demarcated erythematous plaques with thick, silvery scale. Typically on elbows, knees, scalp, and lower back. Nail pitting and onycholysis common. Psoriatic arthritis in up to 30%.
Diagnostic clues: Auspitz sign (pinpoint bleeding after scale removal). Koebner phenomenon. Distinguish from eczema (less well‑defined, fewer silvery scales, more itch).
Standard management: Topical corticosteroids + vitamin D analogs. Phototherapy (narrowband UVB). Systemic agents (methotrexate, cyclosporine, biologics) for moderate‑to‑severe.

## Rosacea
Clinical features: Persistent centrofacial erythema, telangiectasias, and recurrent flushing. Inflammatory papules and pustules (no comedones). May involve eyes (ocular rosacea). Phymatous changes in longstanding cases (rhinophyma).
Diagnostic clues: Blush easily, triggers include sunlight, spicy food, alcohol, stress. Absence of comedones distinguishes from acne. Check for ocular symptoms.
Standard management: Topical metronidazole, ivermectin, azelaic acid. Oral tetracyclines for inflammatory lesions. Laser for telangiectasias. Avoid triggers.

## Seborrheic keratosis
Clinical features: Benign, waxy, “stuck‑on” appearing papule or plaque, ranging from flesh‑colored to dark brown. Surface is greasy and verrucous. Usually multiple, on trunk, face, and extremities. Increase with age.
Diagnostic clues: Keratin plugs (pseudofollicular openings). “Thrown‑on” appearance. May be irritated but not premalignant. Dermoscopy shows milia‑like cysts and comedo‑like openings.
Standard management: Reassurance. Cryotherapy or curettage if symptomatic or for cosmesis. No malignant potential.

## Urticaria (hives)
Clinical features: Pruritic, well‑circumscribed, raised, erythematous wheals that blanche with pressure. Lesions appear and resolve within 24 hours (individual wheals <24 h). May have angioedema.
Diagnostic clues: Wheals are transient and migrate. No scaling or vesicles. Trigger history (food, drug, infection, physical stimuli). Chronic if >6 weeks.
Standard management: Antihistamines (second‑generation H1 blockers) first line. Add H2 blocker or leukotriene receptor antagonist if refractory. Short course oral corticosteroids for severe acute episodes. Identify and avoid triggers.

## Warts (verruca vulgaris)
Clinical features: Hyperkeratotic, rough, dome‑shaped papules with black dots (thrombosed capillaries). Most common on hands, fingers, and knees. Plantar warts on soles, often painful.
Diagnostic clues: Loss of normal dermatoglyphics. Pinpoint bleeding when pared. HPV infection, often in children and young adults. Spontaneous resolution may occur.
Standard management: Salicylic acid preparations first line. Cryotherapy every 1–3 weeks. Imiquimod for resistant warts. No need for referral unless atypical, giant, or immunocompromised.
